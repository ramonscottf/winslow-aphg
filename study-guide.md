# UNIT 1: THINKING GEOGRAPHICALLY

## Chapter 1: Basic Concepts

### What Is Geography?
- **Geography** — the study of where things are and why they are there
- **Human geography** — how people make places, organize space, interact with environment
- **Physical geography** — natural processes (climate, landforms, ecosystems)

### Key Concepts
- **Place** — unique location with physical and human characteristics
  - **Toponym** — name of a place
  - **Site** — physical characteristics of a place (climate, terrain, water)
  - **Situation** — location relative to other places
- **Region** — area with shared characteristics
  - **Formal (uniform) region** — everyone shares a trait (French-speaking Canada)
  - **Functional (nodal) region** — organized around a center (TV market area, commuter zone)
  - **Vernacular (perceptual) region** — exists in people's minds ("the South," "Midwest")
- **Scale** — level of detail (local → regional → national → global)
- **Globalization** — increasing connections between places worldwide
- **Space** — the physical gap between two objects
- **Connection** — relationships between places (spatial interaction)

### Maps & Tools
- **GIS (Geographic Information System)** — computer system for capturing, storing, analyzing spatial data
- **GPS (Global Positioning System)** — satellite-based location finding
- **Remote sensing** — acquiring data about Earth from satellites/aircraft
- **Map projections** — ways to show round Earth on flat surface; all distort something
  - **Mercator** — preserves shape, distorts size (Greenland looks huge)
  - **Robinson** — compromise projection
  - **Equal-area** — preserves size, distorts shape
- **Map types:**
  - **Reference map** — shows locations (road map, political map)
  - **Thematic map** — shows patterns of a specific theme
  - **Choropleth map** — uses shading to show data by area
  - **Dot distribution map** — dots represent occurrences
  - **Isoline map** — lines connect equal values (contour, temperature)
  - **Cartogram** — distorts area based on a variable (population)
  - **Proportional symbol map** — symbol size = data value

### Spatial Thinking
- **Distribution** — arrangement of features in space
  - **Density** — frequency of occurrence per unit area (arithmetic, physiological, agricultural)
  - **Concentration** — spread of features (clustered vs. dispersed)
  - **Pattern** — geometric arrangement (linear, random, regular)
- **Spatial association** — degree to which things are similarly distributed
- **Diffusion** — spread of ideas/innovations from one place to another
  - **Relocation diffusion** — spread through physical movement of people (migration)
  - **Expansion diffusion** — spreads outward from a source:
    - **Hierarchical** — spreads to important places/people first (fashion, tech)
    - **Contagious** — spreads to everything nearby equally (disease)
    - **Stimulus** — underlying idea spreads, adapted by new cultures (McDonald's → local fast food)

### 🔑 Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Site | Physical characteristics of a place |
| Situation | Location relative to surroundings |
| Formal region | Defined by uniform characteristic |
| Functional region | Organized around a node/center |
| Vernacular region | Perceived/mental region |
| Scale | Level of geographic analysis |
| GIS | Computer system for spatial data |
| Spatial association | How distributions relate |
| Diffusion types | Relocation, hierarchical, contagious, stimulus |
| Density | Frequency per unit area |
| Concentration | Clustered vs. dispersed |
| Choropleth map | Shaded thematic map |

# UNIT 2: POPULATION & MIGRATION

## Chapter 2: Population & Health

### Population Distribution
- **Ecumene** — inhabited portion of Earth's surface
- Majority of world population clustered in:
  - **East Asia** (China, Japan, Korea)
  - **South Asia** (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh)
  - **Europe**
  - **Southeast Asia**
- Most people live on **low-lying land** near **oceans** and **rivers**

### Population Density Measures
- **Arithmetic density** — total population ÷ total land area
- **Physiological density** — population ÷ arable (farmable) land → indicates pressure on farmland
- **Agricultural density** — # of farmers ÷ arable land → indicates farming efficiency

### Population Growth
- **Natural Increase Rate (NIR)** — % growth per year = CBR − CDR
- **Crude Birth Rate (CBR)** — births per 1,000 people
- **Crude Death Rate (CDR)** — deaths per 1,000 people
- **Total Fertility Rate (TFR)** — average # children per woman
  - Replacement level = **2.1**
- **Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)** — deaths of infants <1 year per 1,000 live births
- **Life expectancy** — average years a newborn can expect to live
- **Doubling time** — years for population to double (≈ 70 ÷ NIR)

### Demographic Transition Model (DTM) ⭐
THE most important model for the AP exam.

| Stage | CBR | CDR | NIR | Pop Growth | Example |
|-------|-----|-----|-----|------------|---------|
| 1 | High | High | Low/zero | Slow | Pre-agricultural (historical) |
| 2 | High | Rapidly falling | High | Very rapid | Sub-Saharan Africa, some of S. Asia |
| 3 | Falling | Low | Moderate | Slowing | Latin America, parts of Asia |
| 4 | Low | Low | Low/zero | Slow/stable | US, Europe, Japan |
| 5 (debated) | Very low | Low | Negative | Declining | Japan, Russia, parts of Europe |

- **Stage 1 → 2**: CDR drops (better medicine, sanitation, food)
- **Stage 2 → 3**: CBR drops (urbanization, women's education, contraception)
- **Stage 3 → 4**: CBR approaches CDR
- **Why CDR drops first**: Medical technology diffuses faster than cultural change

### Epidemiologic Transition Model
Describes changing causes of death through stages:
1. **Pestilence & famine** — infectious disease, poor nutrition
2. **Receding pandemics** — improved sanitation, fewer epidemics
3. **Degenerative diseases** — heart disease, cancer (aging population)
4. **Delayed degenerative** — medical advances extend life
5. **(Possible)** — reemergence of infectious disease (AIDS, superbugs)

### Population Policies
- **Pro-natalist** — encourage births (France, Sweden — parental leave, child subsidies)
- **Anti-natalist** — discourage births (China's one-child policy → now relaxed)

### Thomas Malthus
- **Malthusian theory** — population grows geometrically, food grows arithmetically → disaster
- **Neo-Malthusians** — agree; worry about resource limits
- **Critics** — technology increases food production; population growth slows naturally (DTM)

## Chapter 3: Migration

### Types of Movement
- **Circulation** — short-term, repetitive movement (commuting, seasonal)
- **Migration** — permanent move to a new location
- **Immigration** — moving INTO a country
- **Emigration** — moving OUT of a country
- **Net migration** — immigration − emigration

### Ravenstein's Laws of Migration
- Most migrate short distances
- Long-distance migrants tend to go to major cities
- Each migration stream has a counter-stream
- Rural residents more likely to migrate than urban
- Women migrate more within a country; men more internationally

### Push & Pull Factors
- **Push** — reasons to leave origin (poverty, war, disaster, persecution)
- **Pull** — reasons to go to destination (jobs, freedom, family, climate)
- **Intervening obstacles** — barriers between origin and destination (ocean, border, cost, language)
- **Intervening opportunity** — an acceptable closer destination

### Types of Migration
- **Voluntary** — choice to move (economic opportunity)
- **Forced** — compelled to move (refugees, slavery)
- **Internal migration** — within a country
  - **Interregional** — between regions (rural → urban, Rust Belt → Sun Belt)
  - **Intraregional** — within a region (city → suburb = counterurbanization)
- **International migration** — between countries
  - **Economic migrants** — seeking better jobs/wages
  - **Refugees** — fleeing persecution, war, disaster
  - **Asylum seekers** — requesting refugee status
  - **IDP (Internally Displaced Person)** — forced to flee but stays in own country

### US Immigration History
- **Pre-1840s**: British, West African (forced)
- **1840s-1880s**: Irish, German ("Old Immigration")
- **1880s-1920s**: Southern/Eastern European ("New Immigration")
- **1920s-1965**: Quota laws restricted non-Western European
- **1965-present**: Latin American, Asian (family reunification, diversity lottery)

### Key Concepts
- **Chain migration** — migrants follow family/friends who came before
- **Step migration** — series of shorter moves (village → town → city)
- **Brain drain** — educated people leave developing countries
- **Remittances** — money sent home by migrants → HUGE economic impact
- **Guest workers (Gastarbeiter)** — temporary foreign workers (Turkish workers in Germany)
- **Unauthorized immigration** — entering without legal permission
- **Refugee camps** — temporary settlements for displaced people

### 🔑 Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Push/pull factors | Reasons to leave / reasons to go |
| Intervening obstacle | Barrier to migration |
| Voluntary vs forced | Choice vs compulsion |
| Refugee vs IDP | Crosses border vs stays in country |
| Chain migration | Following established migrant networks |
| Brain drain | Loss of educated workers from developing countries |
| Remittances | Money sent home by migrants |
| Net migration | Immigration minus emigration |
| Step migration | Series of shorter moves |
| Guest workers | Temporary labor migrants |

# UNIT 3: CULTURAL PATTERNS & PROCESSES

## Chapter 4: Folk & Popular Culture

### Folk Culture vs Popular Culture
| Feature | Folk Culture | Popular Culture |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Origin | Anonymous, tradition | Known source, innovation |
| Diffusion | Slow, relocation | Rapid, hierarchical |
| Distribution | Clustered, isolated | Widespread, global |
| Landscape | Unique, local materials | Uniform, commercial |

### Folk Culture
- **Folk culture** — traditional practices of small, homogeneous groups
- Spreads through **relocation diffusion** (migration)
- Stays **isolated** in specific regions
- Examples: Amish buggy, Himalayan yak butter tea, folk music
- **Folk housing** — built with local materials, reflects climate + tradition
  - US folk house types: New England saltbox, "I-house," shotgun house

### Popular Culture
- **Popular culture** — practices adopted by large, heterogeneous societies
- Spreads through **hierarchical diffusion** (media, internet)
- Creates **uniform landscapes** (same stores, restaurants everywhere)
- Examples: fast food, jeans, social media, pop music
- **Placelessness** — loss of uniqueness as popular culture homogenizes landscapes

### Cultural Landscape
- **Cultural landscape** — visible imprint of human activity on the natural landscape
- Reflects values, beliefs, and practices of the people living there

### Threats & Challenges
- **Cultural imperialism** — dominance of one culture over another (Americanization)
- **Loss of local diversity** — folk traditions replaced by global popular culture
- **Environmental impact** — popular culture demands more resources (fast fashion, electronics)
- **Taboo** — a cultural restriction on behavior (food taboos vary globally)

## Chapter 5: Language

### Language Basics
- **Language** — system of communication with rules
- **Language family** — collection of related languages from a common ancestor
- **Language branch** — subdivision of a family
- **Language group** — further subdivision of a branch
- **Dialect** — regional variation of a language

### Major Language Families
- **Indo-European** — ~45% of world's speakers
  - Branches: Germanic (English, German), Romance (Spanish, French), Slavic (Russian), Indo-Iranian (Hindi)
- **Sino-Tibetan** — ~20% (Mandarin Chinese)
- **Niger-Congo** — Sub-Saharan Africa
- **Afro-Asiatic** — Arabic, Hebrew
- **Austronesian** — Southeast Asia, Pacific Islands
- **Dravidian** — Southern India (Tamil, Telugu)

### Language Diffusion
- **Proto-Indo-European** — hypothetical ancestor language
  - **Kurgan hypothesis** — spread from Central Asian steppes by nomadic herders
  - **Anatolian hypothesis** — spread from Turkey with agriculture
- **Lingua franca** — language used for communication between groups with different native languages (English globally, Swahili in East Africa)
- **Pidgin** — simplified language for trade between groups
- **Creole** — pidgin that becomes a native language

### Language & Identity
- **Official language** — used by government
- **Literary tradition** — language has a written form
- **Isolated language** — unrelated to any other (Basque)
- **Extinct language** — no native speakers remain
- **Endangered language** — few remaining speakers
- **Multilingual states** — multiple official languages (Switzerland, Belgium, Canada)
- **Monolingual states** — one dominant language

### English
- **Global lingua franca** — used in business, science, aviation, internet
- **British colonialism** spread English to North America, Australia, India, Africa
- **Dialects**: British English, American English, Australian English + regional accents

## Chapter 6: Religion

### Universalizing vs Ethnic Religions
| Feature | Universalizing | Ethnic |
|---------|---------------|--------|
| Mission | Actively seek converts | Don't proselytize |
| Spread | Diffusion to new areas | Stay with ethnic group |
| Holy places | Related to founder's life | Related to natural features |
| Examples | Christianity, Islam, Buddhism | Hinduism, Judaism, Shinto |

### Major Universalizing Religions
**Christianity** (~2 billion)
- Branches: **Roman Catholic**, **Protestant**, **Eastern Orthodox**
- Diffused through colonialism, missionaries (relocation + hierarchical)
- Hearth: Palestine/Israel

**Islam** (~1.5 billion)
- Branches: **Sunni** (~85%) and **Shia** (~15%)
- **Five Pillars**: faith, prayer, charity, fasting, pilgrimage (hajj)
- Hearth: Arabian Peninsula (Mecca, Medina)
- Diffused through conquest and trade (contagious + relocation)

**Buddhism** (~400 million)
- Branches: **Mahayana** (East Asia), **Theravada** (Southeast Asia), **Vajrayana/Tibetan**
- **Four Noble Truths**, **Eightfold Path**
- Hearth: Northeast India (Siddhartha Gautama)

### Major Ethnic Religions
**Hinduism** (~1 billion)
- No single founder, oldest major religion
- **Caste system**, karma, dharma, reincarnation
- Concentrated in India and Nepal
- Holy places: Ganges River, Varanasi

**Judaism** (~15 million)
- First monotheistic religion
- Torah, synagogue, rabbi
- Concentrated in Israel and US
- **Diaspora** — scattering of Jews from Palestine

**Other**: Confucianism, Daoism (East Asia), Shinto (Japan), Animism (indigenous)

### Religious Conflict & Landscape
- **Interfaith conflict** — between religions (Crusades, Israel-Palestine)
- **Intrafaith conflict** — within a religion (Sunni vs Shia, Protestant vs Catholic in N. Ireland)
- **Religious landscape** — churches, mosques, temples, cemeteries shape the built environment
- **Secularism** — declining influence of religion in public life (growing in Europe)
- **Fundamentalism** — strict adherence to religious principles
- **Sharia law** — Islamic legal framework

## Chapter 7: Ethnicity

### Key Concepts
- **Ethnicity** — identity with a group sharing cultural traditions + homeland
- **Race** — identity based on perceived biological/physical differences (socially constructed)
- **Racism** — belief in racial superiority
- **Nationality** — identity with a nation/country

### Ethnic Distribution in the US
- **African Americans** — concentrated in Southeast, urban North (Great Migration 1910s-1970s)
- **Hispanic/Latino** — Southwest (Mexican heritage), Florida (Cuban), New York (Puerto Rican)
- **Asian Americans** — concentrated in West Coast, Hawaii
- **Native Americans** — reservations mainly in West

### Ethnic Conflict & Segregation
- **Segregation** — physical separation of groups
- **Apartheid** — South Africa's legal racial segregation system (ended 1990s)
- **Ethnic cleansing** — forced removal of an ethnic group from an area
- **Genocide** — deliberate killing of an ethnic group (Holocaust, Rwanda, Bosnia)
- **Balkanization** — fragmentation of a state along ethnic lines (Yugoslavia)
- **Centripetal forces** — unite a state (shared language, religion, national identity)
- **Centrifugal forces** — divide a state (ethnic conflict, economic inequality)

### Ethnic Neighborhoods
- **Ethnic enclaves** — neighborhoods dominated by one ethnic group (Chinatown, Little Italy)
- Form through **chain migration** and desire for cultural community
- Can provide support but also reflect/reinforce segregation

# UNIT 4: POLITICAL ORGANIZATION OF SPACE

## Chapter 8: Political Geography

### States & Nations
- **State (country)** — sovereign territory with defined borders and government
- **Nation** — group of people with shared cultural identity
- **Nation-state** — state whose territory matches a nation's homeland (ideal, rare: Japan, Iceland)
- **Multinational state** — contains multiple nations (UK, Russia, Belgium)
- **Multistate nation** — nation spread across multiple states (Kurds across Iraq, Turkey, Syria, Iran)
- **Stateless nation** — nation without its own state (Kurds, Palestinians, Rohingya)
- **Sovereignty** — supreme authority within territorial boundaries
- **Self-determination** — right of a nation to govern itself

### Boundary Types
- **Physical/natural boundaries** — rivers, mountains, lakes (Rio Grande, Pyrenees)
- **Cultural boundaries** — based on language, religion, ethnicity
- **Geometric boundaries** — straight lines, often drawn by colonizers (Africa, US-Canada 49th parallel)
- **Antecedent boundary** — drawn before significant settlement
- **Subsequent boundary** — drawn after cultural landscape developed (India-Pakistan)
- **Superimposed boundary** — forced on existing cultural landscape by outsiders (colonial Africa)
- **Relic boundary** — no longer functions but visible on landscape (Berlin Wall)
- **Frontier** — zone of separation between states (less common today)

### Boundary Disputes
- **Definitional** — disagreement over treaty language
- **Locational** — disagreement over where boundary actually is
- **Operational** — disagreement over how boundary functions (immigration policy)
- **Allocational** — disagreement over resources crossing boundary (water, oil)

### Shapes of States
| Shape | Description | Example | Pro/Con |
|-------|-------------|---------|---------|
| Compact | Nearly circular | Poland, Zimbabwe | Efficient communication |
| Elongated | Long and narrow | Chile, Vietnam | Hard to govern extremes |
| Prorupted | Compact with extension | Thailand, Myanmar | Extension can cause conflict |
| Fragmented | Multiple pieces | Indonesia, Philippines | Isolation of parts |
| Perforated | Contains another state | South Africa (Lesotho) | Internal sovereignty issues |

### Types of Government
- **Unitary state** — power concentrated in central government (France, China)
- **Federal state** — power shared between central + regional governments (US, Germany, India)
- **Democracy** — citizens have political power
- **Autocracy/Theocracy** — rule by individual or religious authority

### Geopolitics & International Organizations
- **Colonialism** — establishing control over foreign territory (European empires)
- **Imperialism** — extending power through diplomacy or military
- **Neocolonialism** — economic control without direct political control
- **UN** — promotes international cooperation (193 member states)
- **EU** — economic/political union of European states
- **NATO** — military alliance
- **NAFTA/USMCA** — North American trade agreement
- **Devolution** — transfer of power from central to regional government (Scotland, Catalonia, Quebec)
- **Supranationalism** — states cooperate, giving up some sovereignty (EU)
- **Terrorism** — use of violence by non-state actors for political goals
- **Gerrymandering** — drawing voting districts to benefit one political party
  - **Packing** — concentrating opposition voters into few districts
  - **Cracking** — splitting opposition voters across many districts

# UNIT 5: AGRICULTURE & RURAL LAND USE

## Chapter 9: Food & Agriculture

### Agricultural Origins
- **First Agricultural Revolution (Neolithic)** — ~10,000 years ago
  - Shift from hunting/gathering to farming
  - **Hearths of agriculture**: Fertile Crescent, East Asia, Mesoamerica, Southeast Asia, Ethiopia
  - Led to sedentary settlements, population growth, social stratification
- **Second Agricultural Revolution** — 1700s-1800s (paralleled Industrial Revolution)
  - Mechanization, crop rotation, seed drill
  - Increased food production → supported urbanization
- **Third Agricultural Revolution (Green Revolution)** — 1960s-present
  - High-yield seeds, fertilizers, irrigation, pesticides
  - Dramatically increased food production (especially wheat, rice in India, Mexico)
  - Concerns: dependence on chemicals, water depletion, monoculture

### Subsistence vs Commercial Agriculture
| Feature | Subsistence | Commercial |
|---------|------------|------------|
| Purpose | Feed family | Sell for profit |
| Labor | Intensive | Mechanized |
| Farm size | Small | Large |
| Location | Developing countries | Developed countries |
| Inputs | Low technology | High technology |

### Types of Subsistence Agriculture
- **Shifting cultivation (slash-and-burn)** — clear forest, farm, move on; tropics (Amazon, SE Asia, Africa)
  - **Swidden** — land cleared by slashing/burning
- **Pastoral nomadism** — herding animals across vast areas; arid regions (Sahel, Central Asia, Middle East)
  - **Transhumance** — seasonal movement between pastures
- **Intensive subsistence (wet rice)** — rice paddies, high labor, monsoon Asia
- **Intensive subsistence (non-wet rice)** — wheat, barley in drier parts of Asia
- **Plantation** — large-scale commercial farming of single crop in tropics (coffee, rubber, sugar)

### Types of Commercial Agriculture
- **Mixed crop and livestock** — crops + animals, crop rotation (US Midwest, Europe)
- **Dairy farming** — near urban markets (milkshed)
- **Grain farming** — wheat, corn on large farms (Great Plains, Ukraine, Argentina)
- **Livestock ranching** — cattle on extensive land (US West, Argentina, Australia)
- **Mediterranean agriculture** — olives, grapes, fruits (California, Southern Europe)
- **Commercial gardening (truck farming)** — fruits, vegetables for market (US Southeast)

### Key Models & Concepts
- **Von Thünen Model (1826)** ⭐ — agricultural land use in concentric rings around a market:
  1. **Market gardening/dairy** (closest — perishable)
  2. **Forest** (fuel/building material, heavy)
  3. **Field crops** (grain)
  4. **Ranching/livestock** (farthest — needs most land)
  - Assumptions: flat terrain, single market, uniform transportation
  - Modified by: rivers, roads, soil quality
- **Agribusiness** — large-scale, corporate farming
- **GMO** — genetically modified organisms (controversy over safety, patents)
- **Organic farming** — no synthetic chemicals
- **Fair trade** — better prices for developing-country producers
- **Food desert** — area with limited access to affordable, nutritious food
- **Aquaculture** — farming fish and seafood
- **Desertification** — land becomes desert-like (overgrazing, drought)

## Chapter 10: Development

### Measuring Development
- **Development** — improving material conditions through economic growth
- **More Developed Countries (MDCs)** — high income, industrialized
- **Less Developed Countries (LDCs)** — lower income, agricultural
- **HDI (Human Development Index)** — UN measure combining:
  - Life expectancy
  - Education (literacy + enrollment)
  - Income (GDP per capita)
- **GDP (Gross Domestic Product)** — total value of goods/services produced in a country
- **GDP per capita** — GDP ÷ population
- **GNI (Gross National Income)** — GDP + income from abroad
- **PPP (Purchasing Power Parity)** — adjusts income for cost-of-living differences
- **Gender Inequality Index (GII)** — measures gender-based disadvantage
- **Gini coefficient** — measures income inequality (0 = perfect equality, 1 = total inequality)

### Development Models
- **Rostow's Modernization Model (1960)** — 5 stages of economic growth:
  1. **Traditional society** — subsistence agriculture
  2. **Preconditions for takeoff** — infrastructure, commercial farming begins
  3. **Takeoff** — rapid industrialization, urbanization
  4. **Drive to maturity** — diversified economy, technology
  5. **Age of mass consumption** — high incomes, consumer economy
  - Criticism: Western-centric, assumes single path, ignores colonial legacy
- **Wallerstein's World Systems Theory** — global economy has:
  - **Core** — wealthy, industrialized countries that exploit others (US, Europe, Japan)
  - **Periphery** — poor countries providing raw materials and cheap labor
  - **Semi-periphery** — in between (Brazil, India, China)
  - Criticism: too focused on economics, ignores cultural factors
- **Dependency theory** — LDCs are poor BECAUSE of exploitation by MDCs
  - Colonialism structured economies to export raw materials, not develop locally

### Development Strategies
- **Self-sufficiency (balanced growth)** — develop all sectors simultaneously (India pre-1990)
- **International trade** — focus on export industries (Asian Tigers: South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong)
- **Structural adjustment** — IMF/World Bank loans with conditions (cut subsidies, privatize)
- **Microfinance** — small loans to entrepreneurs in developing countries (Grameen Bank)
- **Foreign direct investment (FDI)** — investment by foreign companies
- **Fair trade** — ensuring producers in LDCs get fair prices

### Sectors of the Economy
| Sector | Activity | Example |
|--------|----------|---------|
| Primary | Extract raw materials | Farming, mining, fishing |
| Secondary | Manufacturing | Factories, construction |
| Tertiary | Services | Retail, healthcare, education |
| Quaternary | Information/knowledge | Research, IT, consulting |
| Quinary | Decision-making | Government, nonprofit leadership |

# UNIT 6: INDUSTRIALIZATION & ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT

## Chapter 11: Industry & Manufacturing

*(See detailed coverage below)*

### Key Issue 1: Where Is Industry Distributed?
- Industrial Revolution: Britain → Europe → North America → East Asia
- Major regions: Europe, North America, East Asia, emerging (India, Brazil, Mexico)

### Key Issue 2: Situation Factors
- **Bulk-reducing** → near inputs (copper smelting, steel)
- **Bulk-gaining** → near market (soft drinks, baking)
- **Break-of-bulk point** — goods transfer between modes (ports)

### Key Issue 3: Site Factors
- **Labor** — #1 factor globally; cheap labor attracts manufacturing
- **Land, capital, energy**
- **Weber's Least Cost Theory** — minimize transport + labor costs
- **Agglomeration** — clustering benefits; **deglomeration** — overcrowding pushes firms out

### Key Issue 4: Changing Location Factors
- **Fordism** → **Post-Fordism** (flexible, just-in-time)
- **NIDL** — manufacturing shifts to developing countries
- **Outsourcing, offshoring, right-to-work laws, maquiladoras**

### 🔑 Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Break-of-bulk point | Transfer point between transport modes |
| Bulk-reducing/gaining | Location near inputs vs. markets |
| Weber's Least Cost Theory | Minimize transport + labor costs |
| Agglomeration/deglomeration | Clustering vs. spreading out |
| Fordism/Post-Fordism | Mass production vs. flexible production |

# UNIT 7: CITIES & URBAN LAND USE

## Chapter 12: Services & Settlements

### Three Types of Services
- **Consumer** — retail, health, education, leisure
- **Business (producer)** — financial, professional, transport/info
- **Public** — government (military, postal, infrastructure)

### Central Place Theory (Christaller) ⭐
- **Market area/hinterland** — area served; hexagonal shape
- **Range** — max distance people travel
- **Threshold** — min people needed to support service
- Nesting hierarchy: small towns → cities → metropolises

### Settlement Patterns
- **Clustered, dispersed, linear** rural settlements
- **Rank-size rule** — 2nd city = ½ largest; 3rd = ⅓, etc.
- **Primate city rule** — largest > 2× second (Paris, London, Mexico City)
- **Gravity model** — interaction ∝ population / distance

### Global Cities
- Command centers for world economy (New York, London, Tokyo)
- **Basic vs non-basic industries** — export vs local

## Chapter 13: Urban Patterns

### Three Models of Urban Structure ⭐⭐
| Model | Creator | Structure |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| Concentric Zone | Burgess (1925) | Rings from CBD outward |
| Sector | Hoyt (1939) | Wedges along transportation |
| Multiple Nuclei | Harris & Ullman (1945) | Several activity centers |

### Zones in Concentric Model
1. CBD  2. Transition  3. Working class  4. Middle class  5. Commuters

### Modern Urban Models
- **Galactic/Peripheral model** — edge cities, beltways, scattered development
- **Edge city** — major employment/retail center outside CBD

### Inner-City Issues
- **Filtering** — housing passes to lower income over time
- **Redlining** — illegal denial of loans by neighborhood
- **Blockbusting** — agents exploit racial fears for profit
- **Gentrification** — higher income renovates inner city → displacement
- **Urban renewal** — demolish and rebuild deteriorated areas
- **Public housing** — government-built low-income housing

### Suburban Issues
- **Sprawl** — low-density peripheral development
- **Smart growth** — anti-sprawl policies
- **New Urbanism** — walkable, mixed-use, transit-oriented design
- **Zoning** — land-use regulations
- **Exclusionary zoning** — large lots/single-family only → keeps out poor

### Developing World City Models
- **Latin American (Griffin-Ford)** — CBD → elite spine → periferico squatter settlements
- **African** — multiple CBDs (colonial, traditional, modern)
- **Southeast Asian (McGee)** — no dominant CBD, corridor-based commerce
